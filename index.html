Freese Guidance

Practical questions, answered from the handbook

Guidance is organized around field decisions rather than chapter headings.

When should I stratify?

When the population can be divided into meaningful groups whose units are more similar within groups than across the population. The sizes or relative sizes of the strata should be known independently of the observations used to estimate their means.

Why does the confidence interval matter?

A sample estimate by itself does not show how much sampling variation to expect. Standard error and confidence limits provide the practical indication of reliability that should accompany the estimate.

When is systematic sampling risky?

Systematic layouts are operationally attractive and common in forestry, but error estimation can be misleading if the population contains spatial patterns related to the grid. Freese discusses both the practical appeal and the statistical caution.

Should I move an inconvenient plot?

Not casually. Selection and field-location procedures should prevent crews from favoring easy terrain or vegetation. A moved or excluded point should be documented, and the replacement process should preserve the sampling design.

When does two-stage sampling make sense?

When locating a sampling area is expensive but taking additional observations once there is relatively inexpensive. The number of primary locations and secondary observations can then be optimized using information about cost and variability.

Permanent or temporary plots for estimating change?

The choice depends strongly on the correlation expected between initial and later measurements. High positive correlation favors permanent plots; low correlation, major cutting, mortality, or long intervals may reduce that advantage.

Publication & Credit

Frank Freese is the foundation of Freese Frame.

Elementary Forest Sampling was written by Frank Freese of the Southern Forest Experiment Station, USDA Forest Service, and published as Agriculture Handbook No. 232 in December 1962. The handbook was reviewed and approved for reprinting in 1976.

Freese described the work as a practical statistical cookbook for foresters. Freese Frame carries that practical objective into an interactive browser application: design a survey, determine the necessary sample, lay it out spatially, bring the data back, and evaluate the estimate and its reliability.
